## Madison arrival: real-person product-check series

Proposal: an owned TikTok/Instagram series called “Check Before Checkout.” A real person explains a real product decision; Derive supplies supported facts/personal fit; the person decides whether that information helps. The story is the purchase decision, not “look how clever our AI is.” This is a distribution experiment, not a proven acquisition channel.

### First shoot: practice without pretending

Invite three to five consenting adult students/friends/club members to bring products they already own or genuinely plan to buy. Start in a private space where the owner has authorized promotional filming. Friends are legitimate participants; don't present recruited friends as surprise strangers or script their praise. Identify beta testers/relationships where relevant. Ask for their product names beforehand to inspect catalog coverage, not to fabricate results or exclude every difficult case.

Target is a later location experiment, not the default first shoot. No blanket official Target guest/commercial filming permission policy was verified in this research. Ask the store/appropriate corporate contact for authorization before filming promotional interviews. A customer's consent does not authorize the location; a parking lot is not an automatic workaround. If permission is unavailable, keep the permissioned private-space format.

Madison parks are not a permit-free fallback for this brand campaign: the city requires a Commercial Shoot Park permit for promotional videos and asks for applications at least 14 days ahead. Don't characterize a Derive advertisement as an exempt student film merely because the founders are students. For campus rooms or street/sidewalk activity, confirm the relevant site's rules rather than assuming access. [Madison filming requirements](https://www.cityofmadison.com/parks/special-events/parks/filming-photo-shoots), [street-use requirements](https://www.cityofmadison.com/parks/special-events/streets/street-use-permit).

### Six repeatable formats

1. **Check before checkout:** “What did you choose, and why?” Show the exact product, one useful supported result, and their genuine decision. No pressure to change their mind.
2. **TikTok made me consider it:** Check a popular product against the participant's voluntarily supplied context. Popular doesn't mean bad; personal fit is not a universal safety verdict.
3. **The shelf regret:** “What did you buy and barely use?” Explain their own reported reason, then demonstrate what Derive can actually help check. A reaction does not prove which ingredient caused it.
4. **Keep what works:** A participant's existing product may already fit. An honest “you don't need to buy something new” is a useful story without manufacturing shopping demand.
5. **Budget decision:** Compare two supported exact products against a stated need and price. Don't claim that an ingredient list proves the cheaper product performs identically or better.
6. **One week later:** Did they use the app again? Did it make a decision clearer? Follow actual behavior, not a seven-day skin transformation claim. When evidence is insufficient, include an honest unknown rather than inventing a result.

No face/skin scores, public flaw rankings, “toxic product exposed,” diagnosis, guaranteed results, or fear-based hooks. Don't promise a scanner can identify a person's allergy from one product reaction. Participants need not expose medical history, pregnancy answers, skin photos, or private account information on camera.

### One episode, 30–45 seconds

- First 3 seconds: specific dilemma—“Would you spend $35 on this moisturizer?”
- Next 8–12 seconds: their reason for choosing it and what they want from it.
- Next 10–15 seconds: real app screen recording and one plain-language, evidence-supported explanation.
- Next 5–8 seconds: “Does that change your decision?” Keep an honest yes, no, or uncertainty.
- Last 3 seconds: one CTA—“Check a product you already own. Try the free beta: [working link].” Mention three free daily checks and scan/link inputs only when implemented and verified; otherwise describe the actual available offer.

Use the real release, not a mock result. Never present generic ingredient information as personalized fit. Condense waiting time if needed without implying a speed claim the app cannot meet. If the result fails, don't substitute an invented result. Capture failure/unknown feedback for the product team.

### Quality and permission checklist

Two founders, two phones (camera plus app/screen recording), quiet background, daylight, and clear close-mic audio. Borrow a microphone if available; expensive gear isn't required. Vertical framing, readable captions, consistent simple styling, and one useful takeaway beat synthetic voiceovers, stock footage, exaggerated reactions, and frantic editing. Hide notifications and crop personal details from recordings.

Obtain clear written permission to record and publicly post the participant's image/voice for Derive promotion; explain where it will appear. Paid advertising/reuse rights need separate explicit agreement. Don't film minors or identifiable nonparticipants; let people decline or stop without buying anything. These are planning safeguards, not a substitute for reviewing the actual release/location terms.

Use TikTok's commercial-content disclosure setting for Derive promotional posts. Affiliate creators must disclose their commercial relationship too. Prefer original spoken audio; if using music, choose commercially authorized music and check rights separately before cross-posting. [TikTok disclosure guidance](https://support.tiktok.com/en/business-and-creator/creator-and-business-accounts/promoting-a-brand-product-or-service), [commercial music guidance](https://support.tiktok.com/en/business-and-creator/creator-and-business-accounts/commercial-use-of-music-on-tiktok).
